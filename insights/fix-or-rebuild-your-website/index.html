When to Fix a Website and When to Rebuild It

A disappointing website does not automatically need replacing. Some can be repaired and some cannot. Here is how to tell the difference before you pay for either.

By Ken AsheEditorial draft, November 24, 2026. Not yet published.7 min read

A website that disappoints its owner does not automatically need to be replaced. Some can be repaired. Some cannot. The deciding factor is usually not how the site looks. It is whether the problem sits on the surface, where it can be repaired, or in the foundation, where repairs keep failing.

This guide gives you a way to tell the two apart before you pay for either.

Start with the complaint, not the cure

"We need a new website" is a cure. The complaint underneath it is often one of these:

  • It looks dated.
  • It is slow, or awkward on a phone.
  • We do not show up in search.
  • We cannot change anything without calling someone.
  • It no longer describes what we do.

The first three are often repairable. The last two more often point toward a rebuild. Write your own complaint down in plain words before you talk to anyone who sells websites, including us.

What can usually be fixed

Appearance. Type, spacing, color, and photography can change a great deal without touching structure. If the pages are organized sensibly and the templates are sound, a visual refresh is a design job, not a rebuild.

Speed. Slow pages usually come from a short list of causes: oversized images, too many third-party scripts, fonts loaded badly, and layouts that jump as they load. Google publishes the thresholds it considers good. By those thresholds, a page's main content should appear within 2.5 seconds, the page should respond to a tap or a click within 200 milliseconds, and its layout shift score should stay at 0.1 or less. You can measure all three on your current site today, and they can often be fixed on it.

Keep speed in proportion. Google says these Core Web Vitals are used by its ranking systems. It also says it "always seeks to show the most relevant content, even if the page experience is sub-par." Fast pages help people. They do not rescue thin content.

Accessibility basics. Text that is hard to read and buttons that are hard to tap are common and repairable. The current Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, known as WCAG 2.2, ask, at Level AA, for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 for normal text and tap targets of at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels.

Content and on-page SEO. An unclear headline, a missing page for a service you sell, weak page titles, and pages that do not link to each other are content problems. A new site built around the same content inherits them.

Signs the foundation is the problem

You do not control it. If you cannot get into the hosting account, do not hold the domain registration, or cannot export your content, the site is rented. That is a business risk whatever the design looks like.

The structure no longer fits the business. One long page doing the work of ten. Services buried in a menu that grew by accident. Page addresses nobody chose on purpose. When what you sell has changed, moving words around inside the old structure is often not enough.

The phone version is an afterthought. Google "uses the mobile version of a site's content, crawled with the smartphone agent, for indexing and ranking." If the mobile site hides content or was bolted on later, that is the version being judged.

Every fix breaks something else. Layers of plugins, page-builder markup nobody understands, and templates patched for years add up to technical debt. You see it as quotes that keep coming in larger than expected and small changes that take weeks.

The platform sets the ceiling. Some site builders will not let you control page addresses, redirects, structured data, or what loads on the page. If the repairs you need are ones the platform does not allow, repairs stop there.

Five questions to guide the decision

  1. Do you control the domain, the hosting, and the content? If not, fix that first. It may force a rebuild by itself.
  2. Does the page structure match what you sell today? List your services and your pages side by side.
  3. Can the current templates be made fast, accessible, and good on a phone? Ask for measurements, not opinions.
  4. Can someone change the site safely and at a reasonable cost? If every change is an adventure, the answer is no.
  5. What is the site already earning in search? Look at which pages bring visitors before you put them at risk.

Treat these questions as practical guidance, not a validated scoring system. They do not replace a proper review of your site. As a rule of thumb, if the answers to the first four are mostly yes, repairing in order of impact is usually the sensible first step. If two or more are a firm no, a rebuild is worth costing seriously, because continued patching may cost more over time. The fifth question does not choose between the two. It tells you how carefully a rebuild has to be handled.

If you rebuild, protect what you have

A rebuild is one of the riskier moments in a site's life for search visibility, and much of that risk comes from page addresses changing without a plan. Google's guidance on moving a site is specific:

  • Keep addresses the same wherever you can. An address that does not change needs no redirect, which lowers the risk. It does not remove it. Google notes that "with any significant change to a site, you may experience ranking fluctuations while Google recrawls and reindexes your site."
  • Where they must change, "prepare a URL mapping from the current URLs to their corresponding new format."
  • Use permanent, server-side redirects from each old address to its new one, and "keep the redirects for as long as possible, generally at least 1 year."
  • Update internal links and the sitemap to the new addresses, and watch both the old and the new addresses in Search Console.
  • Do not change everything at once. For a site that is changing its domain, its platform, and its layout, Google's advice is to "do them one at a time."

Even a careful move has a cost. Google says visibility "may fluctuate temporarily during the move" and that rankings "will settle down over time." Anyone who promises a rebuild with no effect on search is promising something Google does not.

A first-party example. LuckyDomains.io moved from Wix to plain static files in June 2026. Its host cannot issue server-side redirects, so duplicate addresses are handled with canonical tags and consistent internal links. Old addresses that changed later were left in place as instant redirect pages, which Google's documentation describes as an alternative where server-side redirects are not possible. When the site was redesigned in late 2026, every existing address was kept, which reduced the migration risk without removing it. The decisions are public in the site's repository. This is our own site, not client work.

What a rebuild will not fix

A new site does not create demand, reviews, or a reason to choose you. It does not replace content you have not written. Google suggests asking of any page, "Does the content provide substantial value when compared to other pages in search results?" If the honest answer is no, new templates will not change it.

A rebuild also cannot promise rankings. It can remove what was holding the site back and give good content a sound place to live. Both are worth paying for. Neither is a guarantee.

A sensible order of work

  1. Measure. Page speed on a phone, the Page indexing and Performance reports in Search Console, and a plain list of every page you have.
  2. Fix what is cheap and low risk. Images, page titles, broken links, missing pages, contrast.
  3. Decide with evidence. If the five questions still point to a rebuild, you now have a much clearer idea of what the new site has to do better.
  4. Migrate carefully. Keep addresses, map the ones that change, redirect them, and monitor.

The short version. Repair a site whose structure is sound and whose problems are on the surface. Rebuild a site you do not control, cannot change safely, or that is organized around a business you no longer run. Either way, decide from measurements, and treat your existing addresses as an asset.
