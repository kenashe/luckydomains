What AI Search Reads When It Reads Your Website

AI systems do not all read websites the same way. Here is what Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Perplexity say they do, and what that means for your pages.

When an AI system answers a question about your business, the answer came from somewhere. Often it came from pages that a search engine or an AI company's own crawler had already read, including yours.

It is tempting to treat "AI" as one reader with one set of rules. It is not. This guide sets out what the companies themselves have published about how they read websites, what that means for your pages, and where your control ends.

Three different jobs: indexing, retrieval, and training

Most confusion about AI and websites comes from mixing up three separate activities.

Google describes retrieval, which it calls grounding, as "looking up relevant, fresh information from the Google Search index when answering a prompt," and says it "is completely independent of the training step." That independence matters. Being left out of training does not remove you from answers, and being in a model's training data does not mean it will cite you.

Each company runs separate crawlers

Because the jobs are different, most AI companies publish a different crawler name for each. These are their own descriptions.

The settings are independent. OpenAI's documentation gives the example that a site "can allow OAI-SearchBot in order to appear in search results while disallowing GPTBot." It also states the consequence of blocking the wrong one: sites opted out of OAI-SearchBot "will not be shown in ChatGPT search answers." If a developer or a security plugin has blocked "AI bots" as a group, it is worth checking which ones.

What Google says its AI features need

Google has been unusually direct. Its guidance says its generative AI features "are rooted in our core Search ranking and quality systems," and that to be eligible a page "must be indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet." It also describes a Search Console setting, the Search generative AI control, that lets an owner exclude a site from these features. A site that has been excluded there is not eligible.

Google is equally direct about what you do not need:

  • No special files. "You don't need to create new machine readable files, AI text files, markup, or Markdown." On files such as llms.txt, it says "Google Search ignores them," while noting it is fine to keep them for other services that use them.
  • No special schema. "Structured data isn't required for generative AI search, and there's no special schema.org markup you need to add." Google still recommends structured data as part of normal SEO.
  • No chopping pages into fragments. "There's no requirement to break your content into tiny pieces for AI to better understand it."

One detail is useful. Google says these features may issue "multiple related searches across subtopics and data sources" for a single question. A page that answers one specific question well can be found by a search the visitor never typed.

What makes a page easy to read

None of this is exotic. It is the same work that makes a site good for people.

It can be reached and stored. The page is not blocked, is not marked noindex, and is linked from other pages on your site. Even then, Google says it "doesn't guarantee that it will crawl, index, or serve your page."

The words are in the page. Google runs JavaScript when it processes a page, and still calls delivering content from the server "a great idea" because "not all bots can run JavaScript." OpenAI and Perplexity publish nothing on whether their crawlers run it. Anthropic's developer documentation says its web fetch tool "currently does not support websites dynamically rendered with JavaScript," which describes that tool, not its crawlers. A 2024 study by Vercel and MERJ found that none of the major AI crawlers it observed rendered JavaScript. That study is a secondary source and two years old. The safe conclusion is simple: anything that matters should be present in the page as it is first delivered.

The structure is clear. One subject per page, a descriptive title, headings that say what each section covers, and links between related pages. Google says semantic HTML is not required but is "generally a good idea," partly because it helps people using screen readers.

The page says who you are. Your own site should state plainly what the business is, what it does, where, and for whom, and answer the questions customers ask. If your site does not say it, a system has to rely on someone else's version.

Structured data matches the text. Google's rule is "don't add structured data about information that is not visible to the user," and it adds that structured data "does not guarantee" any feature.

The identity is consistent. The same name and description on your site and your official profiles give a system less to reconcile. We cover that in Entity SEO for Founders.

How sources get chosen and cited

No company publishes the rules by which a particular page is selected or cited, and anyone who claims to know them precisely is guessing. What is public is some measurement.

Bing Webmaster Tools has an AI Performance report that shows "how often your content is cited in generative answers, with clear visibility into which URLs are referenced," along with grounding queries, which it defines as "the key phrases the AI used when retrieving content that was referenced in AI-generated answers." Google points site owners to a Generative AI performance report in Search Console.

These reports show what happened on your own site. They do not explain why another source was chosen. Google's guidance also carries a warning worth repeating: "No third-party tool has access to our internal ranking or AI systems."

What you control, and what you do not

A limit worth stating. Nothing in this guide guarantees that an AI system will mention, cite, or recommend a business. It describes what makes a site easier to read and verify. Systems change, and each company's documentation is the authority on its own crawlers.

A short checklist

  • Important pages are indexed. Check Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools
  • Your robots.txt blocks only the crawlers you mean to block
  • Key content is in the HTML, not only added by scripts
  • Each service or topic has its own clearly titled page
  • Your site states who you are, what you do, and where
  • Structured data describes what is visibly on the page
  • Your name and description match on your official profiles
  • You have looked at how AI systems describe you today
